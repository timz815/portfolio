Turning Upcycling Ideas into Achievable Sewing Projects

Upstitch Studio is an app and workshop ecosystem designed for Brother Sewing Machines to support Gen Z beginners interested in sewing and upcycling.

It combines project-specific digital guidance with low-pressure in- person workshops so beginners can explore their own ideas with support throughout the process.

Project Overview

Helping Gen Z beginners start sewing and upcycling.

Upstitch Studio brings together project-specific app guidance and optional in-person workshops for Brother Sewing Machines. The experience helps beginners turn creative ideas into achievable projects while building their skills.

Problem Space

Upcycling is attracting more Gen Z interest, but getting started still requires skills many beginners do not have.

Interest in clothing repair and customization has grown among Gen Z, with 42% saying they want to repair their own clothes. At the same time, 41% report lacking basic repair skills. [1][2]

For someone interested in transforming an existing garment, that skill gap can make sewing difficult to approach without substantial sewing knowledge.

Opportunity

Brother already has the machines and support ecosystem to make sewing more approachable for beginners.

Brother has built its brand around making sewing accessible and supporting users through its “At your side” promise.

Its existing machines, learning resources, and digital ecosystem create a strong foundation for helping prospective users start sewing with more support.

This gives Brother an opportunity to support a growing audience earlier in their sewing journey and introduce them to the brand before they choose a machine.

Research

We looked at what makes sewing difficult for beginners and how Brother currently supports them.

We looked at beginners’ expectations, Brother’s existing support, and how people prefer to learn when they are new to sewing.

Three insights shaped our direction:

Beginners may have ambitious ideas before they have the skills to realize them.

For many beginners, the appeal of upcycling comes from the possibilities. They may know what they want a garment to become without yet understanding the techniques needed to get there.

Research found that dissatisfaction with final results and unmet expectations could lead beginners to abandon sewing altogether. [3]

Existing sewing support is harder to connect to a specific project.

Brother’s existing app, Artspira, already provides sewing guides, machine information, and troubleshooting support.

Its embroidery experience is more closely tied to the design a user is creating, while sewing support often requires users to already know what technique or information they need before they can find the right guidance.

This also helps explain why embroidery is more prominent in the app.

Beginners benefit from learning with someone there to help.

Beginner sewing communities frequently recommend lessons because they make it easier to learn unfamiliar techniques and become comfortable using a machine. [4]

This also aligned with broader interest among young people in in- person communities built around shared interests. [5]

For Brother, these settings also give beginners a chance to try its machines firsthand and become more familiar with how they work.

Design Focus

This led us to ask:

How might we make Brother’s “At your side” promise tangible for Gen Z beginners interested in upcycling?

We needed to balance the freedom to imagine with the structure beginners need to build their skills.

Focusing too heavily on fundamentals could make learning feel tedious. Beginners often already have something they want to make, so working through foundational exercises that feel removed from that goal can make it harder to stay interested.

On the other hand, too much freedom creates the opposite problem. Beginners may have an ambitious idea but not enough experience to break it into something they can actually make.

Gamification helped connect the larger creative goal with smaller, achievable steps along the way. Like progression in a game, beginners still have something bigger they are working toward, but they also have immediate goals that give them a reason to keep moving forward.

Projects are organized along a progress trail, with badges marking what users have completed and new projects unlocking as they build their skills. Each step gives beginners something achievable to work toward while gradually opening up more of the creative freedom they came for.

Workshops needed to make sewing easy to try without putting beginners on the spot.

We initially explored open-ended challenges, such as customizing a top, to make workshops feel more playful and engaging.

User testing showed that this could create more pressure for beginners instead. People who were still figuring out whether sewing was for them often preferred having a clear project to start with rather than being asked to come up with an idea on the spot.

We thus shifted the workshops toward guided projects that give beginners something achievable to make with an instructor nearby. Short pop-ups offer a quick win and easy way to try sewing, while registered workshops provide more time for longer projects as they progress through the app.

Solution Briefing

Upstitch Studio helps beginners start with support, then gives them more freedom as their skills grow.

Upstitch Studio combines guided sewing projects with optional Brother-hosted workshops.

Beginners can try their first projects at a workshop with Brother machines and an instructor nearby, or work through the same projects at home through the app.

Early projects provide more structure while still leaving room for creative choices. As users build their skills, they unlock projects that introduce more advanced techniques and customization tools that give them more control over the outcome.

Solution

Optional workshops make sewing easier to try and give Brother a hands-on way to reach new makers.

Brother-hosted pop-ups bring short Upstitch Studio projects into thrift stores, where people already interested in clothing and upcycling can try sewing without buying a machine or committing to a full class.

Participants use Brother machines and follow the app-guided project with an instructor nearby when they need help. Longer registered workshops give beginners more time and support for harder projects.

The same projects can also be completed at home, while the in-person format gives beginners a more supported way to get started and experience Brother's machines firsthand.

Guided projects build skills without making the experience feel prescribed.

Each project teaches a manageable set of sewing skills while giving users control over parts of the outcome.

A project might let someone choose the shape of a coaster or choose a character for an animal bookmark. These choices let users personalize the result while staying within what the app can turn into a working guide.

This gives beginners enough freedom to make some meaningful choices without pushing the project beyond what their current skills can support.

Support stays connected to the project the user is actually making.

Once users decide what they want to make, Upstitch Studio adapts the rest of the guide around those choices.

The app shows where to cut, how to set up the machine, and what to do next based on the project they configured. Explanations and troubleshooting also stay within the guide when something is unfamiliar.

Because the app already has the context of what they are making, users do not need to know what a technique is called or search through general support to find the right help.

Reflection

Don’t spend too long on a subproblem.

Upstitch Studio had a lot happening at once. We were thinking about workshops, project progression, customization, guidance, and how those pieces connected.

A lot of the directions we explored were worth pursuing. The problem was that once we went deep into one of them, it became harder to tell whether we were still improving the overall experience or just refining one part because there was more we could do with it.

The research gave us something to return to when the solution became complicated. It helped us decide what still needed development, what could be left as-is, and what ideas could be cut despite being promising on their own.
